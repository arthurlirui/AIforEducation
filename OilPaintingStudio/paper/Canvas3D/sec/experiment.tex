\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiment}

We structure the experimental evaluation around four research
questions:
\textbf{Q1}~Does Canvas3D outperform state-of-the-art image-to-video,
3D reconstruction, and painting-animation methods on a curated
painting benchmark?
\textbf{Q2}~How much does each of the four modules contribute to the
final quality, and which design choices within each module are
load-bearing?
\textbf{Q3}~Do human viewers prefer Canvas3D outputs on artistic
authenticity, motion naturalness, and compositional-intent alignment?
\textbf{Q4}~How does the runtime and memory budget decompose across
modules, and where does the computational cost concentrate?

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We curate the \textbf{PaintingAnim-50} benchmark comprising 50
high-resolution digital scans of classical paintings (30 oil, 15
ink-wash, 5 fresco) drawn from both Western and East Asian traditions.
The collection spans diverse compositional types: single-figure
portraits (12), multi-figure narrative scenes (18), landscapes with
architectural elements (12), and animal or subject-centered works (8).
Each painting is annotated with instance-level segmentation masks
(5--15 objects per work), hand-drawn line-art sketches at 3--5 target
compositions produced by two professional illustrators, and
ground-truth 3D camera trajectories for evaluation. The benchmark is
partitioned into 35 training paintings (used for fine-tuning the
segmentation backbone and tuning pipeline hyperparameters) and 15
held-out test paintings on which all reported results are computed.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
All components are implemented in PyTorch. PaintParse employs a
one-stage instance segmentation backbone~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} with
low-rank art-domain adaptation~\cite{hu2022lora} followed by full
mask-head fine-tuning, paired with a ViT-L monocular depth
estimator~\cite{yang2024depthanything}. MonoSplat accesses a
feed-forward single-image 3D predictor~\cite{tripo2025tritosplat}
through its official API. SketchComp's contour registration is
optimized by gradient descent with iterative re-rendering, and
disocclusion inpainting uses a latent inpainting diffusion
model~\cite{rombach2022ldm,lugmayr2022repaint} augmented with a
depth-conditioned control branch~\cite{zhang2023controlnet}. Edge maps
are extracted with HED~\cite{xie2015hed}. ArtiFlow executes video
synthesis through a ComfyUI HTTP API, driving a large-capacity video
diffusion backbone with dual control branches (depth strength
$s_d{=}0.85$, edge strength $s_e{=}0.6$) and first-frame latent
anchoring. The keyframe count is fixed to $T=25$ (i.e., $8N{+}1$ with
$N{=}3$).

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments run on a single 48\,GB GPU. Per-stage peak memory is
approximately 4\,GB (PaintParse), 8\,GB (SketchComp), and 24\,GB
(ArtiFlow); MonoSplat is API-based and thus excluded from the local
memory budget. End-to-end processing time per painting is
approximately 14 minutes, dominated by ArtiFlow (8 min) and SketchComp
inpainting.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against six methods spanning the relevant paradigms.
\emph{Direct I2V} baselines apply two state-of-the-art video diffusion
backbones to the full painting with default parameters.
\emph{Generative-prior clipart animation} performs video score
distillation from text-to-video priors, adapted for painting inputs.
The \emph{2DGS + bilinear upsampling} baseline performs full-scene 2D
Gaussian Splatting reconstruction followed by bilinear interpolation
to video resolution, without diffusion refinement.
\emph{OilPaintingRevive}~\cite{oilpaintingrevive2026} contributes a
multi-stage skeleton-binding pipeline with latent video refinement.
\emph{LivePortrait}~\cite{guo2024liveportrait} is evaluated on the
portrait subset (12 paintings) only, animating the dominant figure in
each work.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report \textbf{CLIP-I} (CLIP image cosine similarity between
generated frames and the original painting, using the CLIP
space of \citeauthor{radford2021clip}~\cite{radford2021clip}) for style
fidelity; \textbf{LPIPS}~\cite{zhang2018lpips} for perceptual quality;
\textbf{FVD} (Fr\'echet Video Distance~\cite{unterthiner2019fvd},
computed with a video-trained I3D backbone) for temporal coherence;
\textbf{Disocclusion PSNR} for inpainted gap quality against a
manually inpainted reference; and \textbf{Composition IoU}, the
average bounding-box overlap between objects detected in generated
frames and the target positions specified by the line-art sketches,
measuring compositional-intent alignment.

\subsection{Main Results (Q1)}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the primary quantitative
comparison. Canvas3D attains the highest style fidelity (CLIP-I 0.86)
and the lowest perceptual distance (LPIPS 0.28), outperforming the
next-best method by 3.6\% and 9.7\%, respectively. Its temporal
coherence (FVD 18.3) improves upon the next-best painting-specific
baseline by 19.7\% and the strongest direct-I2V baseline by 35.6\%.

IoU (0.81), as no baseline supports object repositioning with respect
to external line art. The pure 2DGS baseline yields the lowest CLIP-I
(0.55), confirming that 3D reconstruction without diffusion
refinement is insufficient for paintings.

\subsection{Continuous 3D Scene Evolution (Q1b)}

A defining capability of Canvas3D---and one that no per-frame
conditioning baseline can offer---is the generation of \emph{video
whose underlying 3D scene varies continuously and coherently along a
smooth camera trajectory}. Because every keyframe is rendered from
the composited scene $\mathcal{S}^{*}$ along a B-spline camera path
(Section~\ref{sec:method:artiflow}), geometric quantities such as
binocular disparity, depth ordering, and occlusion relationships
evolve \emph{as continuous functions of the viewpoint} rather than
being re-guessed independently at each frame.
Figure~\ref{fig:scene_evolution} depicts this behavior on a
representative multi-figure test painting: as the virtual camera pans
and dollies from $t{=}1$ to $t{=}25$, the foreground figure exhibits
the largest image-plane displacement, mid-ground architecture drifts
proportionally to its depth, and the background skyline remains nearly
stationary---exactly the depth-graded parallax signature of a coherent
3D scene. Meanwhile, the per-frame rendered depth maps remain
piecewise smooth and temporally ordered, and the occlusion order
between the two figures inverts exactly when the camera crosses the
critical viewing angle, as predicted by the geometry of
$\mathcal{S}^{*}$.

We quantify this continuity with two trajectory-level diagnostics
computed over the 15 test paintings. \textbf{Depth ordering
consistency} measures the fraction of consecutive keyframe pairs for
which the sign of the depth difference between each object pair is
preserved or flips in accordance with the rendered geometry of
$\mathcal{S}^{*}$; Canvas3D achieves 97.2\%, whereas the best direct
I2V baseline attains only 61.4\%---its depth ordering fluctuates
incoherently because no geometric quantity is shared across frames.
\textbf{Parallax monotonicity} measures the Spearman rank correlation
between per-object mean image displacement and the inverse of object
depth (closer objects must move farther under camera motion); Canvas3D
achieves $\rho = 0.91$ against $0.38$ for the direct-I2V baseline.
Together, these diagnostics confirm that the video produced by
Canvas3D is not merely a stylistically coherent sequence of frames but
a faithful projection of a single, continuously varying 3D scene.

\subsection{Ablation Studies (Q2)}

We isolate each module's contribution (Table~\ref{tab:ablations}).

\paragraph{PaintParse: art-domain adaptation.}
Removing the painting-specific fine-tuning (reverting to a
natural-image-only detector) degrades segmentation mAP by 8.3\%. The
error cascades downstream: CLIP-I drops by 5.8\% and Composition IoU
falls by 9.9\%, because imprecise mask boundaries cause MonoSplat to
hallucinate 3D geometry at object--background transitions and force
SketchComp's contour registration to match against degraded
silhouettes.

\paragraph{MonoSplat: feed-forward 3D vs.\ 2DGS back-projection.}
Replacing the feed-forward 3D predictor with 2D Gaussian Splatting
back-projection~\cite{huang20242dgs} causes the largest
single-component degradation: CLIP-I drops by 10.5\% and FVD increases
by 52.5\%. The single-depth back-projection produces flat,
view-dependent surfel geometry that cannot represent occluded object
sides or tolerate the large rotational transformations that
SketchComp's rigid registration imposes. The feed-forward predictor's
generative prior, trained on diverse 3D assets, successfully
generalizes to the missing geometry, validating the design choice of
\emph{lifting with a generative prior} rather than reconstructing from
a single depth map.

\paragraph{SketchComp: depth-aware inpainting and contour
registration.}
Without depth-conditioned inpainting (replaced by patch-match texture
synthesis), LPIPS degrades by 39.3\% and Disocclusion PSNR falls to
21.4\,dB. The depth control branch proves critical: it constrains the
filled content to follow the background's geometric structure,
preventing the texture-smear artifact that naive inpainting produces
on paintings. Removing contour registration entirely (replacing it
with bounding-box alignment) causes a catastrophic 49.4\% drop in
Composition IoU, validating the necessity of iterative silhouette
matching for precise sketch-to-3D alignment.

\paragraph{ArtiFlow: dual control and latent anchoring.}
Removing the edge control branch increases FVD by 52.5\%---the largest
temporal degradation among ArtiFlow ablations---confirming that edge
maps are essential for preserving brushstroke boundary coherence
across frames. Removing the depth control branch produces a 78.1\% FVD
increase and a 14.8\% Composition IoU loss, as the diffusion backbone
loses geometric grounding and objects drift from their intended
positions. First-frame latent anchoring provides a moderate benefit,
contributing 3.5\% to CLIP-I and 31.7\% to FVD, primarily stabilizing
the initial 4--6 frames of each generated sequence.

\subsection{User Study (Q3)}

We conduct a human evaluation with 25 participants drawn from art
practice, art history, and general viewing backgrounds. Each
participant views 20 triplets consisting of a 5-second Canvas3D
output alongside outputs from the strongest direct-I2V baseline and
OilPaintingRevive~\cite{oilpaintingrevive2026} for the same painting,
presented in randomized, anonymized order. Participants rate each
video on three 5-point Likert scales: \textbf{artistic authenticity}
(``How well does the video preserve the painting's artistic
character?''), \textbf{motion naturalness} (``How natural and
physically plausible is the object motion?''), and
\textbf{compositional intent} (``How well does the result match the
intended composition shown in the reference sketch?'').

Results (Table~\ref{tab:user_study}) show Canvas3D leading on all
three dimensions; all pairwise advantages over both baselines are
significant at $p < 0.01$ under a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test
over per-trial ratings. The artistic-authenticity advantage over
OilPaintingRevive (mean 4.31 vs.\ 3.59) reflects the benefit of
object-level rather than full-scene 3D lifting, which better preserves
individual brushstroke identity. The compositional-intent gap is the
largest (4.48 vs.\ unavailable for baselines), as no comparison method
supports sketch-based object repositioning.

\subsection{Compute Analysis (Q4)}

Table~\ref{tab:vram} details the per-stage memory budget and runtime.
ArtiFlow video generation is the primary bottleneck at 24\,GB and 8
minutes, driven by the video diffusion backbone with dual control
injection. MonoSplat's API-based execution accounts for roughly 3
minutes across 5--8 objects. PaintParse and SketchComp are
comparatively lightweight, although the inpainting diffusion step
within SketchComp constitutes the majority of its runtime. The total
wall-clock time of approximately 14 minutes per painting is dominated
by the two diffusion-based stages (SketchComp inpainting and ArtiFlow
video generation), suggesting that inference optimization of these
diffusion passes is the most promising path toward faster end-to-end
performance.
