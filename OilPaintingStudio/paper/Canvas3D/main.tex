\begin{abstract}
Animating classical paintings into explorable videos requires both
artistic fidelity and three-dimensional scene structure, yet existing
methods offer only one or the other. Image-to-video diffusion models
produce fluent motion but cannot maintain consistent 3D structure under
viewpoint change, whereas 3D reconstruction pipelines capture geometry
at the cost of painterly texture. We observe that the two objectives
are hard to satisfy jointly because they favor conflicting
representations, and propose \textbf{Canvas3D}, a framework that
instead factors the problem into four sequentially composed
sub-problems, each handled by a specialist. \emph{PaintParse}
decomposes the painting into foreground objects and a background layer,
ordered by monocular depth; \emph{MonoSplat} lifts each object into a
metric-scaled 3D Gaussian representation and assembles a reusable
object library; \emph{SketchComp} treats hand-drawn line art as a
spatial constraint, placing each 3D object by contour-based rigid
registration and filling disocclusions with depth-conditioned
inpainting; and \emph{ArtiFlow} renders the composited scene along a
camera trajectory and injects depth and edge cues as dual control
signals into a video diffusion backbone, anchoring motion to explicit
geometry. The decomposition supports object repositioning, multi-view
exploration, and style-preserving animation---capabilities that
per-frame conditioning cannot provide. On a benchmark of 50 classical
paintings, Canvas3D outperforms image-to-video, reconstruction, and
painting-specific baselines in style fidelity, perceptual quality, and
temporal coherence, and user studies confirm a clear preference for its
outputs.
\end{abstract}
