\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Yayoi Kusama's polka dot art rests on four visual traits: hard-edged
dots, coexisting dot sizes spanning more than an order of magnitude,
flat high-chroma color fields, and \emph{horror vacui}---the
compulsion to cover every surface with marks
\citep{moma_kusama,kusama2011infinity}. Computational stippling would
seem a natural tool for reproducing this language, yet the two are
mismatched. Classical stippling
\citep{deussen2000floating,secord2002weighted} modulates dot size and
density by a single scalar field, usually luminance: dark regions get
large or dense dots, bright regions get few and small ones. Kusama's
dots do something luminance cannot capture---they carry a sense of
near and far, of the object inhabiting space.

Monocular depth estimation supplies the missing signal. Unified
real-time backbones such as YOLO26 \citep{ultralytics2025yolo26} now
produce dense metric depth and semantic segmentation from a single
feature extractor, and dedicated depth models
\citep{ranftl2022towards,yang2024depth} achieve strong
cross-domain generalization. We adopt YOLO26 for three reasons: a
single model family provides depth and segmentation that are spatially
consistent by construction; inference takes a few seconds at
$1024\times1024$, fitting an artist-in-the-loop workflow; and the
metric output simplifies normalization. The pipeline itself is
backbone-agnostic---any depth estimator and segmenter can be
substituted without algorithmic changes.

Our central device is a depth-to-radius mapping
\begin{equation}
    r(x,y) = \Bigl[\, r_{\min} + (r_{\max} - r_{\min}) \cdot (1 - \hat{d})^\alpha \,\Bigr] \cdot (1 + \varepsilon),
    \label{eq:depth_radius}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{d}(x,y) \in [0,1]$ is foreground-normalized depth (0 =
nearest), $\alpha$ controls the depth--size falloff, and
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ is multiplicative jitter
that mimics the size statistics of hand-painted dots. Near surfaces
receive large dots; distant surfaces recede into small, sparse ones.
Semantic segmentation additionally enables region-aware coloring:
foreground and background dots take different colors within one
duotone palette, echoing Kusama's practice of contrasting dots on
different surfaces.

Two design decisions separate the pipeline from a naive composition
of off-the-shelf components. First, the radius range demanded by the
depth mapping (roughly 1--15\,px) far exceeds what a single-layer
stipple supports without blob merging; we therefore decompose
rendering into three layers---fine stipple, depth-emphasis, and
edge/background---each operating in a distinct radius regime. Second,
a cross-layer pruning step enforces a minimum separation between the
large emphasis dots and the fine stipple, which the ablation study
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) identifies as the component most
critical to output quality.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A depth-to-radius mapping (Eq.~\ref{eq:depth_radius}) that
    encodes scene depth into dot size with controlled randomization,
    giving flat polka dot renderings a perceptible
    three-dimensional structure.
    \item A three-layer stippling architecture with cross-layer
    overlap pruning that maintains dot separation (maximum blob
    ratio below 0.65\%) over a wide radius range (0.5--15.4\,px).
    \item Semantic-aware dual-color rendering from a unified YOLO26
    backbone, assigning distinct dot colors to foreground and
    background within a Kusama-style palette.
    \item A learnable depth-to-radius network trained with a
    self-supervised perceptual stippling loss, offering a data-driven
    alternative to the fixed power-law mapping.
\end{enumerate}
